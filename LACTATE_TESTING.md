## 3. Why mid-December

### 3.1 Not before the Asia trip

**Shelf life.** 17 days of walking only, in a trained cyclist, costs something real. Coyle et al.
(1984), on highly-trained endurance athletes, found VO₂max down ~7% over the first 12–21 days —
roughly half of it from reduced plasma volume and stroke volume rather than anything muscular.
Mujika & Padilla's short-term detraining review puts oxidative enzyme activity down ~12–25% over
2–4 weeks, with blood lactate at a fixed submaximal power measurably higher within 2–3 weeks.
Expect to come back ~5–8% down on threshold power — call it 15–20 W off a 240 W eFTP.

**The bigger problem: it would measure the wrong athlete.** As of September 2026 I'm four weeks
into a hill-climb block — VO₂/anaerobic-heavy, volume trimmed. That sharpens glycolytic capacity,
which raises lactate at any given submaximal power and tends to *depress* measured LT1 relative to
where it sits in an aerobically-based state. A curve taken then would describe a
glycolytically-sharpened, low-volume athlete, and would be used to prescribe a high-volume aerobic
base block. Not a stale number — a misleading one.

**And there is no "test while fit" window anyway.** Before 4 Oct is race taper and shouldn't be
disturbed. After 5 Oct is deliberately unstructured and already drifting down.

### 3.2 Not in the first fortnight back

Weeks 1–3 back capture a transient state that gets blown straight past. Plasma volume restores
within days to ~2 weeks of retraining; mitochondrial and enzymatic recovery takes more like 4–6
weeks. Zones set in week 1 would be meaningfully under-prescribed by week 3 — which is exactly the
situation a lab test is supposed to end.

### 3.3 Why ~week 4 is the sweet spot

By mid-December the fast-moving components have settled and there's enough consistent aerobic work
in the legs that the curve is stable rather than sliding. Numbers taken then should hold reasonably
for 8–10 weeks, covering January–February — the block where a correct LT1 matters most, because the
entire volume distribution of a base block depends on knowing where the top of true Z2 actually is.

It also lands before Christmas, so January starts with real zones.

**Weeks 1–3 do not need lab precision.** Re-aerobicisation off RPE and HR, deliberately
conservative. Precision there buys nothing.

## 5. Booking checklist — hand this to the lab

**Protocol.** Most labs default to 3-minute stages with 25–30 W steps. That resolves LT2 acceptably
and smears LT1 — three minutes isn't long enough for capillary lactate to settle at submaximal
loads. Ask for:

- [ ] **4–5 minute stages**, **20 W increments**
- [ ] **Start at 100–110 W** (~45% of eFTP). Needs 3–4 clean data points *below* LT1 — you cannot
      locate a first rise without knowing what it's rising from. Expected ladder:
      110 / 130 / 150 / 170 / 190 / 210 / 230 / 250 / 270 …
      (LT1 likely lands somewhere around 160–190 W, giving 3–4 points underneath it.)
- [ ] **Ramp to exhaustion**, so LT2 and VO₂max come out of the same test

**Outputs.**

- [ ] **The raw stage-by-stage table** — power, HR, lactate, RPE, gas data. *This is the single most
      important ask and the one people forget.* Labs hand over a zone chart generated by one
      analysis method; the raw numbers allow re-analysis (log-log, Dmax, +0.3 mmol over baseline)
      and, critically, a like-for-like comparison with any future test.
- [ ] LT1, stated **with the method used to identify it**
- [ ] LT2 / MLSS estimate, method stated
- [ ] **HR at both thresholds**, not just power
- [ ] **Gas exchange if offered** — VO₂max plus VT1/VT2 alongside lactate is far more informative
      than lactate alone, and the marginal cost is usually small. The RER-derived fat/CHO oxidation
      curve feeds fuelling strategy directly, which matters for the long-ride direction of 2027.

**Ask whether a repeat-test package is discounted.** If so, the highest-value pair is
mid-December + late February, showing whether the base block actually moved LT1.

## 6. The 48 hours before

Standardise it, because the point is to be able to repeat it.

- **Fed, not fasted.** Glycogen depletion suppresses lactate and right-shifts the whole curve —
  it would flatter the thresholds and be wrong. Normal carbohydrate intake for 24h, carb meal
  2–3h before.
- **No hard training for 48h.** Easy spin or rest the day before. Arrive with TSB around 0.
- **No alcohol for 24h.**
- **Standardise caffeine** — usual dose or none, but record which.
- **Same time of day** as any future repeat.
- **Record everything**: bike, position, shoes, pedals, what was eaten, sleep, body mass.

- [ ] Either **ride my own bike + Kickr** if the lab permits,

perfectly. **LT1 HR and LTHR come back usable regardless of whose power meter measured them** — and
those are exactly the numbers currently unverified. Only the power figures need cross-calibration.

## 9. Home lactate meter — buy or not?

**Conditionally yes, but decide after the lab test, not before.**

Handheld meter (Lactate Plus, Lactate Pro 2): £200–350, strips £1.50–2.50 each.

**What it buys that power data cannot:**

1. **LT1 drift checks** mid-block, without lab cost — an abbreviated version of the lab protocol
   every 6–10 weeks
2. **MLSS confirmation** — 30 minutes at a target power, checking the rise from minute 10 to minute
   30 stays under ~1.0 mmol. The most directly actionable home use; settles "is my threshold
   actually where I think it is" in one session.
3. **Durability testing** — lactate at a fixed power when fresh versus after 3 hours. The most
   interesting thing available to measure for a long-ride-shaped 2027, and nothing else I own
   measures it.

**The honest downsides:**

- Analytic CV around 5–10%, and **pre-analytical error is usually larger** — sweat contamination on
  the fingertip is the big one. Wipe thoroughly with a dry cloth, discard the first drop.
- **Strip-lot variation** makes cross-lot comparisons unreliable. Buy one large lot and stay on it.
- At three casual tests a year the noise exceeds the signal and the result is chasing artefacts.

**Decision rule:** buy it only with a commitment to a fixed, repeatable protocol used regularly.
Skip it if the goal is mainly zones — a 30-minute TT plus intervals.icu eFTP tracking covers LT2
adequately, and LT1 is what the lab visit is for.

**Does buying one change the December timing?** No, but it adds free optional data. Buying early
would allow a self-test before the trip and another in week 1 back, purely to watch the rebuild
trajectory — with the December lab test as the calibrated anchor that says whether the home
technique produces sane numbers. **That ordering matters: the lab test validates the home meter,
not the other way round.** It also means learning the finger-prick technique while the stakes are
zero, which is worth something — the first few attempts are usually bad.

**What not to do:** buy a meter *instead* of the lab test. Without a reference curve, home numbers
come with no way of knowing whether they're right.

## 10. Sources

- Coyle EF et al. (1984) — *Time course of loss of adaptations after stopping prolonged intense
  endurance training.* J Appl Physiol. VO₂max −7% over 12–21 days; early loss largely plasma
  volume / stroke volume.
- Coyle EF et al. (1985) — Longer detraining; mitochondrial enzyme decline, disproportionate fall
  in lactate threshold.
- Mujika I & Padilla S (2000) — *Detraining: loss of training-induced physiological and performance
  adaptations. Part I (short term).* Sports Med. Plasma volume, submaximal lactate, enzyme kinetics.

> Figures above are population estimates from trained-athlete cohorts, quoted as ranges. They are
> the basis for the *timing* decision, not predictions about specific personal numbers.
